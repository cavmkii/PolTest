Which bad leader, and which philosopher, is closest to you?

The political test matches you to 49 real ideologies and 50 dictators, militants and notorious figures. The philosophy test matches you to 34 schools of thought and 78 philosophers, from Confucius and Aquinas to Husserl, Derrida and Rorty. Take both and you also see where your philosophy and your politics pull apart.

                              Method

                              Axes

                              What the axes are, and are not

                              The ten axes are a display choice, not a finding. Research on mass political attitudes repeatedly finds that they sort mainly onto two correlated dimensions, economic and cultural/authoritarian (Feldman & Johnston 2014; Treier & Hillygus 2009), and that the two relate differently across countries (Malka, Lelkes & Soto 2019). Culture, Religion, Membership and Justice probably measure overlapping things. This test has never been checked against real response data, so no axis here has an established reliability.

                              State power mixes authoritarian disposition, which the literature measures separately as right-wing authoritarianism (Altemeyer 1996), social dominance orientation (Pratto et al. 1994), child-rearing authoritarianism (Feldman & Stenner 1997) and left-wing authoritarianism (Costello et al. 2022), with civil-liberties policy. The statements are worded as policy so the axis leans toward the second.

                              Populism is treated as a thin ideology that attaches to a host ideology (Mudde 2004), not as a point in this space. Measuring it properly needs its own scale (Akkerman, Mudde & Zaslove 2014; Castanho Silva et al. 2020), which this test does not include.

                              Scoring

                              Answers count +1, +½, 0, −½, −1. "No opinion" is separate from Neutral (Krosnick et al. 2002) and drops the statement. Each statement carries a weight on one or more axes; your axis score is the weighted sum over the largest possible sum, scaled to −100…+100 (the 8values model). Each axis has statements keyed in both directions to limit acquiescence bias. Double-barreled statements were split or reworded. One attention-check statement is not scored.

                              The violence statements describe concrete acts rather than abstract principles. Westwood, Grimmer, Tyler & Nall (2022) showed abstract items overstate support for political violence. Even so, Means is the least trustworthy axis and counts half as much as the others in matching.

                              Matching uses weighted root-mean-square distance across all ten axes, the standard form in spatial models of political choice (Enelow & Hinich 1984). The "Matters" setting on each axis changes its weight. A blank axis on a profile counts as a fixed gap (35 points for figures, 30 for ideologies), so sparsely documented profiles do not win by default. When the top ideologies are within 3 points, the result says there is no clear fit. Percentages are shown as approximate because the profiles themselves are estimates.

                              Answer consistency reports how often answers on the same axis point the same way. Most people do not hold tightly constrained ideological views (Converse 1964; Kinder & Kalmoe 2017); a low score is common and means the nearest ideology tells you less.

                              How the results are explained

                              The ideology notes describe each ideology from its standard sources. "Your positions in practice" is built from your axis scores, not from the label. The psychological section uses research on the two broad dimensions (economic and cultural/authoritarian), because almost no research exists on the psychology of named ideologies. The exceptions (libertarians, Christian nationalists, populist-right voters, jihadist radicalization) are cited where they apply. All of it describes group averages with modest effect sizes and cannot tell you about yourself.

                              The philosophy test

                              The nine philosophy axes follow the structure found in the PhilPapers Surveys of professional philosophers (Bourget & Chalmers 2014, 2023). In the 2009 survey, libertarian free will, non-physicalism about the mind and theism loaded together on one "anti-naturalism" component (loadings around 0.63–0.66); other components were objectivism/Platonism, rationalism and externalism. Reality, Knowledge and Truth here follow those components. Morality, Right action and Moral scope follow the standard divides in metaethics and normative ethics, and impartiality follows Kahane et al.'s (2018) separation of impartial beneficence from instrumental harm. Freedom, Self and Method cover free will, personal identity and the analytic/continental divide. Freedom and Reality are kept apart, though the survey found them correlated, because compatibilism (59% of philosophers in 2020) cuts across the theism question. Under each axis the results show how professional philosophers answered the closest survey question.

                              Each of the 78 philosophers and 34 schools is scored from primary works, listed on each profile, with a standard secondary source where readings differ. Scores for Heidegger, Derrida, Nietzsche, Laozi and others whose positions resist propositional summary are marked medium confidence. Some axes are left blank where a thinker took no position or the reading is too contested. Many philosophers fit no school neatly, and the school list is a map, not a taxonomy.

                              A script that answers the test as each fully scored philosopher would puts that philosopher in their own top three in every case, in both versions. That shows the scoring is internally consistent, not that the profiles are right. Two statements' secondary weights tilt Truth slightly toward Construction (+6.2 against −3.5 in the full test), so readings near the middle of that axis should be taken loosely.

                              Comparing philosophy and politics

                              When both tests are taken, four links between philosophical and political views are checked, each backed by published research: free-will belief and punitiveness (Carey & Paulhus 2013; Shariff et al. 2014; Everett et al. 2021); theism and religion in public life (Bourget & Chalmers 2014); moral objectivism with religiosity and intolerance of disagreement (Goodwin & Darley 2008; Wright, Cullum & Schwab 2008); and impartial ethics with identification with all humanity (Kahane et al. 2018). Other plausible links, such as consequentialism and economic policy, were left out because no solid research supports them. A "less common" result means your pairing is rarer than predicted. Each one is paired with a tradition that holds it on principle.

                              How the figures were scored

                              The roster is limited to divisive figures: dictators, militant and terrorist leaders, and notorious public figures. Positions are placed on the present-day spectrum you answer on. Each profile lists the sources it was checked against.

                              • Economy for heads of state since 1945 was checked against Herre's (2023) Global Leader Ideologies dataset. Where the codes disagreed with the first draft, the score moved: Lee Kuan Yew (coded leftist), Khomeini (rightist), Duterte (rightist), Assad (leftist) and Idi Amin (non-ideological, now blank).
                              • Party positions for Erdoğan, Bolsonaro, Putin, Chávez, Mugabe, Thatcher, Lee Kuan Yew and Saddam were checked against V-Party expert codings (Lührmann et al. 2020) for economic position, religious appeals, LGBT equality, immigration, pluralism and encouragement of violence.
                              • Regime type flags come from V-Dem's codings of how a regime legitimizes itself: through the leader's person, through ideology, and which ideology. The Geddes-Wright-Frantz autocratic regimes data and the Archigos leader data were planned for this but could not be downloaded from this environment, so they were not used.
                              • Militant groups follow the group histories and classifications in Stanford's Mapping Militant Organizations, Rapoport's four waves, and the scholarship listed on each profile. al-Qaeda and ISIS are separated as Wiktorowicz and Bunzel separate them.
                              • Celebrities and notorious figures (Combs, Epstein, Ye, Musk, Rogan, Tate) have no scholarly coding. Their profiles rest on journalism and their own statements, they are marked low confidence, and they are excluded from the overall ranking.

                              A close match means you share some stated positions with that person. It says nothing about conduct, and because nobody mainstream is on the list, a moderate will still get a "closest" figure who is much further from them than the name suggests.

                              Not done

                              The test has not been validated: no factor analysis, no reliability estimates (omega or alpha), no test-retest check, no comparison against self-placement or party identification. Those need response data, which this page does not collect. Until then, treat the output as a structured argument about where you sit, not a measurement.
